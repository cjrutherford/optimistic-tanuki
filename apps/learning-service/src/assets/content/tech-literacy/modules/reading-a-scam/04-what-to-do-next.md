# What to Do, and What Not to Do

Recognizing a suspicious message is only the first half of the problem. What
you do immediately afterward matters, because some of the most natural
responses — replying to say stop, clicking an unsubscribe link, engaging to
see what happens — actively make things worse in ways that are not obvious
until explained. This lesson covers the response that limits harm, and what
to do in the specific case where you already acted before recognizing the
message.

---

## Report, Do Not Engage

The general principle is to report a suspicious message through a channel
designed for that purpose — a "report phishing" or "report spam" option in
your email or messaging app, or a fraud-reporting line for your bank or phone
carrier — and otherwise leave it alone. Do not reply, do not click contained
links, do not call a number the message provides, and do not attempt to
engage with the sender to test whether the message is real, out of curiosity
or to see what happens next.

Engagement is exactly what a scam is designed to elicit, and it is valuable
to whoever sent the message regardless of what you say in your reply,
because the content of the reply matters less than the fact that a reply
happened at all.

---

## Why Replying "STOP" Confirms You Are Real

A text message you did not expect, offering to let you opt out by replying
"STOP," or a marketing email with an "unsubscribe" link, appears to offer a
reasonable way to make the messages stop. For a legitimate sender — a company
you actually have a relationship with, sending real marketing — this
mechanism generally works as described and is a normal part of how
opt-outs function, and using it is fine.

For an unexpected message from a sender you do not recognize, or one you
suspect is fraudulent, the same mechanism serves a different function.
Sending any message back — "STOP," a question, an angry reply, anything —
confirms two things to whoever is running the campaign: that your number or
address is currently in use and actively monitored by a person, and that
messages to it get read rather than ignored. A number or address confirmed
this way becomes more valuable to whoever sent the message, and more likely
to be targeted again, including by other campaigns if the confirmed-active
list is sold or shared onward. The same reasoning applies to an unsubscribe
link, which typically requires visiting a page to register the opt-out —
visiting that page confirms your address is live in the same way replying
does, and on a fraudulent message, the "unsubscribe" page itself can be
another attempt to collect information or deliver something unwanted.

The distinction that matters is whether you recognize the sender as a
legitimate one you have an actual relationship with. For an unexpected
message from an unrecognized or suspected sender, using the built-in reporting
function of your email or messaging app avoids sending any message back to
the sender at all, which is why it is the safer path in that specific case.

---

## What Reporting Actually Does

Reporting a message through your email provider or phone carrier's built-in
tool typically does two things: it removes the message from your view, and
it contributes the report to that provider's systems used to identify and
block similar messages more broadly. This differs meaningfully from deleting
a message without reporting it, which removes it from your view only, with
no effect on whether the same message reaches someone else.

---

## If You Already Clicked

If you have clicked a link, entered credentials into a page you now suspect
was fraudulent, or provided information in response to a call or message,
the priority shifts from prevention to limiting the consequences of what
already happened, and acting promptly matters more than any other single
factor at this stage.

If you entered a password on a page you now believe was fake, change that
password on the real site directly — reached by typing the address yourself
or using a bookmark you already had, not by any link connected to the
suspicious message — and change it as well on any other account where you
reused the same password, since a password entered into a fake page is
compromised for every account it was used on.

If you provided financial account information or made a payment, contact the
financial institution directly, through a number from an independent,
already-trusted source, to report the specific transaction or account
exposure and ask about reversal or additional protections available for that
situation.

If you provided information that could support identity theft more broadly —
a government identification number, a full date of birth combined with other
identifying details — this generally warrants a report to the relevant
consumer protection or identity-theft authority in your country, which
typically provides specific next steps calibrated to what was exposed.

None of these steps depend on figuring out exactly how the scam worked or
who was behind it. They depend on identifying what specifically was given
away, and addressing that specific exposure directly and promptly.

---

## Telling Someone Else

Having acted on a fraudulent message, or nearly having done so, is a common
experience rather than a rare failure of judgement — the techniques covered
in this module are effective specifically because they are engineered to
work on careful, competent people under the right conditions of time
pressure and plausible framing. Telling a colleague, a family member, or an
employer's IT or security contact what happened serves a purpose beyond
personal relief: the same message is frequently sent to many people at once,
and an early report can prompt a warning to others before they encounter the
same one, or before they act on it without the benefit of having seen it
described already.

Workplaces in particular benefit from this being normalized rather than
treated as an embarrassment to minimize. A phishing message that succeeds
against one employee's account can be used to reach further into an
organization's systems or contact list, so a prompt, unembarrassed report
protects more than the individual who received it.

1. Report a suspicious message through your provider's built-in tool, and otherwise do not engage with it at all.
2. Replying "STOP," answering a question, or visiting an unsubscribe page all confirm your address is active and monitored, on a message from a sender you do not recognize as legitimate.
3. That confirmation makes the address more likely to be targeted again, including by other campaigns.
4. Reporting contributes to broader blocking systems; deleting alone only removes the message from your own view.
5. If you already clicked or provided information, act promptly: change any reused password directly on the real site, contact your financial institution through an independently verified number, and report identity-theft-relevant exposure to the appropriate authority.
