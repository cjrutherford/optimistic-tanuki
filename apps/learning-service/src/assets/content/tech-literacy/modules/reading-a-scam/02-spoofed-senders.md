# Who Actually Sent This

An email or message displays a sender name, and that name is not the same
thing as the address the message actually came from. This gap — between what
is displayed and what is underneath — is where a large share of message-based
scams operate, and it is checkable in a specific, mechanical way once you
know where to look.

---

## Display Name Versus Address

An email has a display name, which is the human-readable text shown in most
inbox views — "Account Security," "Jane at Accounting," "Customer Support" —
and a separate underlying address, the actual string with an @ symbol that
the message was sent from. The display name is a field the sender fills in
freely, with no verification attached to it. Anyone sending an email can set
the display name to whatever text they want, including a name copied
directly from a real organization or a real coworker.

This means a display name reading "Your Bank" is not evidence the message
came from your bank. It is evidence that whoever sent the message typed
"Your Bank" into the display-name field. The address underneath is the part
that identifies where the message actually originated, and it is usually
hidden by default in most email programs — visible only after clicking or
tapping on the sender's name, or hovering over it, depending on the program.
Checking it is a habit worth building specifically because it is one extra
step, hidden by default, and skipped by most people most of the time.

---

## Lookalike Domains

The address itself can also be constructed to look correct at a glance while
differing in a way designed to be easy to miss. The part of an address after
the @ symbol is the domain, and a domain that closely resembles a real one —
differing by a single character, an added word, or a different ending — is a
lookalike domain.

Some concrete patterns: a letter substituted for a similar-looking one, an
extra word inserted before the real company name, or the same company name
paired with a different, less common ending than the organization actually
uses. None of these require sophisticated technology to produce — registering
a domain is a routine, low-cost transaction available to anyone, so a
domain's mere existence and superficial resemblance to a real one says
nothing about who controls it.

The reliable check is comparing the domain in front of you, character by
character, against a domain you already know to be correct — one you have
used before, or one printed on a physical document from that organization —
rather than judging it by whether it looks roughly right.

---

## Reply-To Is a Third, Separate Field

Some messages set the visible "from" address to something legitimate-looking
while quietly setting a different "reply-to" address, so that any reply you
send goes to the sender's actual address rather than the one displayed. This
is a distinct field from both the display name and the from-address, checked
separately, and worth knowing about specifically because a message can pass
a check of the from-address while still redirecting a reply elsewhere.

---

## Link Text Versus Link Destination

The same display-versus-underlying gap applies to links inside a message. The
visible text of a link — the blue, underlined words, or a button labelled
"Verify Your Account" — is chosen by whoever wrote the message and can say
anything at all. The actual destination, the address the link will send your
browser to if clicked, is a separate piece of data attached to that same
text, and the two do not have to match.

On most devices, holding a cursor over a link without clicking it, or pressing
and briefly holding a link on a touchscreen, reveals the actual destination
before you commit to visiting it. This is the direct equivalent of checking
an email address rather than trusting a display name: the visible label is
one thing, and the underlying data is a separate thing worth checking on its
own terms.

A link's destination itself can also use a lookalike domain, for the same
reasons and in the same forms described above, so the same character-by-
character comparison applies once the real destination is visible.

---

## What This Does and Does Not Prove

Confirming that a message's address and link destinations genuinely match
the organization they claim to represent is meaningful evidence toward
legitimacy — it rules out the most common and lowest-effort version of
impersonation. It is not absolute proof, because a sender's own account or
domain can occasionally be compromised and used to send genuine-looking
messages from a genuinely correct address. This is uncommon relative to
simple display-name and lookalike-domain impersonation, but it means the
checks in this lesson raise confidence rather than guaranteeing certainty,
and a message making an unusual request is still worth verifying through an
independent channel even when its address checks out.

---

## Building the Habit

None of the checks in this lesson take long once they are routine — checking
a sender's address, comparing a domain character by character, hovering over
a link before clicking it. The reason they feel effortful the first several
times is that they are not the default behavior most email and messaging
programs encourage; most interfaces are designed to get you from message to
action as quickly as possible, which is convenient for the ordinary case and
exactly the gap a fraudulent message is built to exploit.

A reasonable target is not to apply every check to every message — that
would be exhausting and unnecessary for routine correspondence with people
and organizations you already interact with regularly. The reasonable
target is applying these checks specifically to messages that ask you to do
something: click a link, provide information, make a payment, or act before
a deadline. That narrower category is where the checks matter most, and it
is a small enough category that checking it consistently is realistic.

1. A display name is freely chosen by the sender and proves nothing on its own; the underlying address is the part to check.
2. A lookalike domain resembles a real one closely enough to pass a glance, and registering one is cheap and unrestricted.
3. The reply-to address is a separate field from the from-address and can redirect responses without changing what is displayed.
4. A link's visible text and its actual destination are two different pieces of data, checkable by hovering or a long-press before clicking.
5. Matching addresses and destinations is strong evidence, not absolute proof — an unusual request still warrants independent verification.
