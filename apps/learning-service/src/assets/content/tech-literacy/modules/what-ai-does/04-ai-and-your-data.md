# What Happens to What You Type

Every message you send to a chatbot leaves your device and goes somewhere
else before an answer comes back. That is true of almost every online tool,
but it's worth walking through deliberately here, because the habit of
typing freely into a text box — pasting a paragraph, a document, a
question with real details in it — is easy to form and hard to reverse once
something sensitive has already been sent.

---

## Where a Prompt Actually Goes

When you send a message to a chatbot, it travels to the company that
operates the service, is processed there to generate a response, and
travels back. That much is common to essentially every online chatbot,
because the model doing the generating runs on the company's computers, not
on your device, in the overwhelming majority of consumer products.

What happens to your message after that point — whether it's stored,
for how long, whether anyone at the company can read it, whether it's used
to improve the product or train future versions of the model — depends
entirely on the specific product, its specific settings, and the agreement
you accepted, explicitly or by clicking through, when you started using it.
This is not one answer across all products. It varies by company, by
product tier, by account settings, and it changes over time as products are
updated. A rule that names a specific product and describes its current
policy will be wrong eventually, possibly soon. A rule based on categories
of information holds up regardless of which product you're using or when
you're using it, so that is the shape this lesson takes.

---

## "Retained" and "Reviewed" Are Two Different Things

It's worth separating two possibilities that get blurred together.

Retained means the message is stored somewhere after your conversation
ends — on a server, potentially indefinitely, potentially tied to your
account, potentially used later to train or improve the system. Reviewed
means a person, not just a machine, might read it — sometimes as part of
safety monitoring, sometimes as part of resolving a support request,
sometimes as part of quality checks on how the system is performing.

A message can be retained without being reviewed by a person. It can be
reviewed without being retained long-term. Many products do both. The
point is not to memorize which is worse — it's that "the company has a
privacy policy" is not the same claim as "no human will ever see this" or
"this disappears when I close the window." Assume, by default, that
anything you type may be stored and may be seen by someone other than the
system generating your reply, unless the specific product you're using
states clearly that it doesn't, in a way you've actually gone and read.

---

## A Rule About Categories, Not Vendors

Because the specifics vary by product and change over time, the durable
version of this guidance is about what kind of information you send, not
which company you're sending it to.

Do not paste in client data — names, contact details, case details, or
anything else about another person or organization that was shared with
you in confidence, especially where you have a professional or legal duty
to protect it. Do not paste in credentials — passwords, access keys, account
numbers, anything that would let someone act as you or access a system if
they read it. Do not paste in anything covered by a confidentiality
agreement, a non-disclosure agreement, a data-handling policy at your job,
or a regulation that applies to your industry, without first checking
whether your employer has approved that particular tool for that purpose.

The common thread across all three: once you've sent something to an
external service, you no longer have exclusive control over where it goes,
and you often cannot verify after the fact whether it was retained,
reviewed, or used in some way you didn't intend. That's true whether the
service is a chatbot, a file-sharing tool, or an email provider — it's not
unique to AI products, but chatbots make it easy to forget, because the
interaction feels like a private conversation rather than a submission to
an external server.

---

## What to Do Instead

When you have a genuine need to work through something that involves
sensitive information — drafting a response to a client issue, summarizing
a confidential report, working through a problem that involves real
account details — the workable pattern is to strip the specifics out before
you type. Replace the client's real name with "the client," the real
account number with a placeholder, the specific figures with rounded or
representative ones, and ask your question in the abstract. You usually
still get a useful answer, and none of the sensitive detail ever left your
control.

If your employer has a specific tool that's been reviewed and approved for
handling sensitive information — sometimes companies run or contract a
private version of these tools that comes with different retention terms —
that approval is the thing to check for and rely on, not your own guess
about how any given consumer product handles data this week.

---

## Settings Are Worth Checking, Not Trusting Blindly

Many chatbot products include a setting that limits whether your
conversations are used to train future versions of the system, and
sometimes a separate setting for how long conversations are retained
before being deleted. Where these settings exist, they're worth finding
and setting deliberately rather than leaving on whatever the product
shipped with — the default is often the option that shares more, not less,
because that default tends to benefit the company more than it benefits
you. Finding these settings takes a few minutes the first time and is a
reasonable thing to do for any tool you expect to use regularly.

That said, a setting is a promise about how the company says the product
behaves, not a technical guarantee you can verify yourself from inside the
chat window. Treat an adjusted setting as a meaningful improvement over the
default, not as a reason to abandon the category-based rule above. The
setting changes what's more likely to happen to ordinary conversations. It
does not change what you should be willing to type into the box in the
first place when the content is genuinely sensitive.

---

## In Short

1. A prompt leaves your device and is processed on the company's servers; assume it can be stored and possibly seen by a person.
2. "Retained" and "reviewed" are separate possibilities, and both are common.
3. Terms differ by product and change over time, so treat this as a rule about categories of information, not about specific vendors.
4. Do not paste in client data, credentials, or anything covered by an agreement you've signed.
5. Strip specifics out before asking, or use an employer-approved tool, when the underlying question genuinely involves sensitive information.
