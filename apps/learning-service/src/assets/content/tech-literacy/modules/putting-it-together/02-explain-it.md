# Explain One of These to Somebody Else

There's a reliable way to find out whether you actually understand
something, as opposed to having recognized it while someone else explained
it: try to explain it yourself, to someone who doesn't already know it,
without the original explanation in front of you. The gaps show up
immediately, in a way that re-reading a lesson never quite forces. This
lesson sets up that exercise as the closing activity for the course.

---

## Why Explaining Is a Real Test

Recognizing an explanation as it goes by and reconstructing it yourself are
different skills, and it's easy to mistake the first for the second.
Reading the lesson on backups, nodding along at "synced isn't backed up,"
and feeling like the idea is obvious is not the same as being able to say,
cold, to someone else, why a synced folder doesn't protect against
accidental deletion, in your own words, with an example that actually
holds up. The second one requires you to have rebuilt the idea, not just
followed it while someone else did the work.

This is why teaching is often described as the point at which you find out
what you actually know. It isn't a metaphor — it's a direct consequence of
what explaining requires that reading doesn't.

---

## What a Good Explanation Actually Does

Three things separate an explanation that works from one that doesn't.

It's accurate. Not just roughly gesturing at the right idea, but correct in
the specific claims it makes — if you're explaining what a VPN does, the
explanation should reflect what a VPN actually does and doesn't cover, not
a vague sense that it "makes you private," which this course specifically
addressed and rejected as too broad a claim.

It doesn't lean on unexplained jargon. Using a technical term is fine;
using it without ever cashing it out into a plain description is where an
explanation quietly loses the person listening. If your explanation of
two-factor authentication uses the phrase "two-factor authentication"
without ever explaining what that second factor actually is and why it
helps, you've stated the term, not explained the concept. A useful test:
could this explanation work for someone who has never heard the term
before, without them needing to look anything up partway through.

It says why it matters. A definition alone — "a backup is a copy of your
data kept somewhere else" — is accurate and still incomplete. A good
explanation connects the idea to a consequence the listener would actually
care about: what changes for them if they have this, or don't. The lessons
in this course tried to model that throughout — not just what a thing is,
but what follows from it.

---

## Picking What to Explain

Choose one concept from the course — not the whole course, one specific
idea. Good candidates: what a chatbot is actually doing when it answers a
question and why that makes a wrong answer sound identical to a right one;
the difference between a synced file and a backup; why an update closes a
window rather than being a routine nuisance; what a VPN does and doesn't
cover. Pick something you feel reasonably solid on, since the exercise is
about testing and sharpening real understanding, not about faking your way
through something you're still shaky on.

---

## The Activity

Explain your chosen concept to somebody who has genuinely never encountered
it — not a colleague who already works in a technical role, but someone
whose gap in this area is real, the way this whole course was written for
someone with a real gap rather than someone who needed reassurance that
technology isn't scary. Do it out loud if you can — a spoken explanation
exposes gaps faster than a written one, because there's no chance to quietly
smooth over a part you're unsure of by rephrasing it into something vaguer.

Notice, while you're doing it, whether you reach for a term you can't
actually unpack, or a claim you're repeating without being sure it's true.
Those moments are the useful output of the exercise — more useful, in a
real sense, than a clean explanation delivered without any friction, because
they tell you exactly where to go back and firm something up.

Afterward, write a short account of how it went: what you explained, what
question or confused look told you a part wasn't landing, and what you'd
change about the explanation next time. That written account, alongside the
explanation itself, is what completes the course.

---

## If the Explanation Falls Flat

It's a genuinely useful outcome if the person you explain this to still has
questions afterward, or asks something you can't answer, or tells you
plainly that part of it didn't make sense. That is not evidence the
exercise failed. It's the exercise doing exactly what it's designed to do
— surfacing the specific edge of your own understanding, which is
something a reread of the lesson, on its own, would not have shown you. If
that happens, the natural next step is to go back to the lesson the
concept came from, find the part that covers the gap, and try explaining
that part again, either to the same person or to yourself. Understanding
built this way tends to be sturdier than understanding that was only ever
tested by recognizing the right answer on a page.

---

## In Short

1. Explaining tests understanding in a way recognition and rereading don't.
2. A good explanation is accurate, avoids unexplained jargon, and says why the idea matters.
3. Pick one specific concept, not the whole course, and pick one you're genuinely solid on.
4. Explain it out loud to someone with a real gap in this area, and notice where it gets shaky.
5. Write down what happened — the friction points are the most useful part of the exercise.
