# Audit Your Own Accounts

This lesson is not primarily something to read. It's a structured pass over
your own real accounts and devices, using everything covered in this
course so far. Set aside some time — the first real pass takes longer than
the five-minute monthly check described earlier, because you're building
the baseline that routine will maintain — and work through it in order,
against your actual accounts, not hypothetically.

Keep a simple list as you go: a note of what you checked, what you found,
and what you changed. That list is what makes this an audit rather than
just reading through your settings once.

---

## Part One: List What You Actually Have

Before checking anything, write down what you're checking. Most people
carry more accounts and devices than they'd guess if asked cold. List:

Every device you use regularly — phone, computer, tablet, anything else
that holds your accounts or data. Every account that matters if it were
lost or accessed by someone else — primary email first, since email is
usually how you'd recover everything else, then banking, then anything
storing sensitive work or personal information, then everything else.

This list is the scope of the rest of the audit. Anything not on it won't
get checked, so take a genuine few minutes here rather than rushing to the
parts that feel more active.

---

## Part Two: Passwords and Two-Factor Authentication

For each account on your list, check two things: whether its password is
unique to that account — not reused anywhere else — and whether two-factor
authentication is turned on, meaning signing in requires a second step
beyond the password, usually a code sent to your phone or generated by an
app. A reused password means a breach at one company can be used to access
your account at a completely unrelated one. Two-factor authentication means
a stolen or guessed password alone isn't enough to get in.

Where you find a reused password on an important account, change it before
moving on to the next section — this is the single highest-value action in
the entire audit, so it's worth stopping for rather than noting for later.

---

## Part Three: Connected Apps and Recent Activity

For your primary email and any account holding financial or sensitive
information, work through the same two checks from the monthly routine:
what apps and services have access, and what recent sign-in activity looks
like. This is the same process described in that lesson — the difference
here is depth. This first pass is the one where you actually go through
every entry rather than scanning for something obviously wrong, because
this is the pass establishing what "normal" looks like for your accounts,
which is what makes the quick monthly scan meaningful afterward.

---

## Part Four: Backups

For each device on your list, confirm what backup, if any, actually exists
for it, and whether what you have is a genuine backup or only a synced
folder — the distinction covered earlier in this course. Where a device
has no real backup, this is the point to set one up, not just to note the
gap.

---

## Part Five: Updates

Check every device on your list for pending updates, and check that
automatic updates are turned on where that option exists. Where automatic
updates aren't available, this is also the moment to actually put the
monthly reminder from the earlier lesson onto your calendar, if you haven't
already.

---

## Part Six: Devices You Don't Use Anymore

Old phones, old computers, an account you made once and forgot about —
these often still have access to things, or still hold data, long after
they stopped being part of your regular routine. Part of a real audit is
identifying anything like this on your list and deciding, deliberately,
whether it should be wiped, deleted, or have its access revoked, rather
than left as an unattended loose end.

---

## Pacing Yourself

This does not need to happen in one sitting, and for most people it
shouldn't. Six parts across a real list of accounts and devices is a
genuine chunk of work, and rushing it to finish in one pass tends to
produce the same shallow scanning this audit is meant to replace. Splitting
it across a few evenings — email and passwords one night, backups and
updates another — produces a more thorough result than forcing it into a
single hour. What matters is that every part gets done with actual
attention, not that it happens all at once.

---

## Closing the Loop

When you finish, you should have a list: what you checked, what you
found, and what you changed. Keep that list somewhere you can find it in
a month — it's the starting point for your first monthly check-in, and a
useful record of what a thorough pass through your own accounts actually
involves, for the next time you do one.

---

## In Short

1. Start by listing every device and every account that matters, before checking anything.
2. Fix reused passwords first — it's the highest-value single change available.
3. Turn on two-factor authentication wherever it's offered.
4. Go through connected apps, recent activity, backups, and updates for everything on your list, not just a sample.
5. Decide deliberately what happens to devices and accounts you no longer use, rather than leaving them unattended.
