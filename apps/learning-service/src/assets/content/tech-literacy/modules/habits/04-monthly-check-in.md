# A Five-Minute Monthly Check

Everything in this module so far has been explanation: what backups
actually protect against, why updates matter, what does and doesn't carry
real risk day to day. This lesson converts that into a routine — a short,
repeatable check you run on a schedule, rather than something you only
think about after something has already gone wrong.

Five minutes is a genuine estimate for someone doing this regularly, not an
introductory offer. The first time will take longer, because you're finding
these settings for the first time and possibly discovering things that need
attention. After that, it's largely a glance at a handful of screens you'll
already know how to find.

---

## Step One: Check What Has Access to Your Accounts

Most important accounts — email, banking, social media, cloud storage —
have a settings page listing connected apps and services: things you signed
into using that account, or gave permission to read your data, at some
point in the past, possibly years ago and long forgotten. Look for a
section usually called something like "connected apps," "third-party
access," or "apps and services" in your account's security or privacy
settings.

Scan the list for anything you don't recognize, don't use anymore, or
never meant to grant that much access to. Revoking access for something you
no longer use costs nothing and removes a connection you're not tracking
anymore. This step matters because access granted once tends to stay
granted indefinitely until someone removes it — services rarely ask you to
reconfirm.

---

## Step Two: Glance at Recent Sign-Ins

The same account settings areas usually include a record of recent sign-in
activity — device type, approximate location, and time. You're looking for
one thing: anything that isn't you. An unfamiliar location, a device you
don't own, a time you know you weren't using the account.

You are not trying to memorize this list or investigate every entry in
depth. You're doing a quick pattern check — does this look like a history
of one person, in the places that person has actually been, or does
something stand out. If something does stand out, that's the moment to
change the password and check what else might have been accessed, rather
than something to resolve during this five-minute pass.

---

## Step Three: Confirm a Backup Actually Ran

The backups lesson in this module distinguished a real backup from a synced
folder, and noted that a backup you set up once and never checked on again
is a common way people discover, too late, that it silently stopped
working. This step is that check: open whatever service or drive holds your
backup, and confirm there's a recent date on it — not just that the service
is installed, but that it actually ran recently and the most recent files
you care about are actually in it.

If it hasn't run, this is the point to find out why — a drive that's no
longer connected, an account that logged itself out, a subscription that
lapsed — while it's a five-minute fix, rather than after you've already
lost something and need the backup to exist.

---

## Step Four: Install What's Pending

Finally, check for and install any updates that have been sitting there —
on your phone, your computer, and the handful of apps you use most.
The updates lesson in this module covered why the gap between a fix
existing and you installing it is the period that matters most; this step
is where that turns into an actual, scheduled action instead of something
you keep meaning to get to.

---

## Making It Actually Recur

A routine that lives only as a good intention doesn't run. Putting a
recurring reminder on a calendar — the same day each month, paired with
something else you already do regularly, like paying a bill — is what
turns this from an idea you agreed with into something that actually
happens twelve times a year. The content of the check matters less than
it happening consistently; a five-minute check done every month catches far
more than a thorough audit done once and never repeated.

---

## What to Do When You Find Something

Occasionally this check turns up something that needs more than five
minutes — a sign-in you don't recognize, a backup that's been silently
failing, an account with no two-factor authentication set up at all. When
that happens, the right response is to stop and deal with that one thing
before moving to the next step, not to note it down and continue the scan.
The check is meant to surface problems quickly; it isn't meant to be the
place you also fix everything it finds while staying inside five minutes.
Some months will run long. That's the check working as intended, not a
sign you're doing it wrong.

---

## In Short

1. Review connected apps and third-party access on your important accounts, and revoke what you no longer use.
2. Scan recent sign-in activity for anything that isn't you.
3. Confirm your backup has a recent date on it, not just that it's installed.
4. Install pending updates on your devices and most-used apps.
5. Attach the check to a recurring calendar reminder so it actually happens monthly.
