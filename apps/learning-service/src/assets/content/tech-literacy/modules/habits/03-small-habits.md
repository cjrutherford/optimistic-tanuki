# Public Wi-Fi, Locking, and Other Small Things

Some of the most repeated advice in this area is also somewhat out of date,
carried forward past the point where it stopped matching how things
actually work. This lesson covers a handful of small, everyday habits, and
is deliberately honest about which ones matter and which ones matter less
than folklore suggests, because overstating a risk is its own kind of
inaccuracy — it spends your attention on the wrong things.

---

## What Public Wi-Fi Actually Risks Now

The traditional warning about public Wi-Fi — at a coffee shop, an airport,
a hotel — is that someone else on the same network can watch your traffic
and see what you're doing, or intercept things like passwords as they
travel. That warning was accurate when it was written. It is substantially
less true today, and it's worth understanding why, rather than either
dismissing the warning entirely or repeating it unchanged.

The reason is encryption, specifically the padlock icon and "https" you
see in front of nearly every website address today. Encrypted traffic
between your device and the website you're visiting is scrambled in a way
that a third party on the same network cannot read, even if they can see
that a connection is happening. A decade or more ago, a much larger share
of web traffic was unencrypted, so a person on the same coffee shop network
genuinely could watch what you were typing on certain sites. Today, the
overwhelming majority of everyday browsing, email, banking, and app traffic
is encrypted by default, and has been for some time. That does not mean
public Wi-Fi risk is zero — a minority of sites and older apps still send
some data unencrypted, and a malicious network operator has other tricks
beyond passively watching traffic — but the blanket claim that public
Wi-Fi is broadly dangerous to use for ordinary browsing overstates the
current risk considerably. This is one of the places where the honest
answer is smaller than the folklore.

A separate and real risk on public networks is a fake network with a
trustworthy-sounding name, set up specifically to get you to connect to it
instead of the legitimate one. Checking the exact network name with staff
before connecting, when it matters, addresses that specific risk directly,
and is a more precisely targeted habit than avoiding public Wi-Fi
altogether.

## What a VPN Does and Doesn't Do

A VPN — virtual private network — routes your internet traffic through an
intermediary server before it reaches its destination. Two concrete effects
follow from that: your local network and your internet provider can no
longer see the traffic's actual destination or content, only that you're
connected to the VPN, and websites you visit see the VPN server's location
rather than your own.

What a VPN does not do is remove the VPN provider from the picture — it
now sees what your local network and provider used to see, so you've
relocated that visibility rather than eliminated it, which makes the choice
of VPN provider a real decision rather than a formality. And a VPN has no
effect at all on cookies, logins, or device fingerprinting — the ways
websites and apps recognize you and track your activity once you're
signed in or browsing, which have nothing to do with which network route
your traffic took to get there. A VPN is a useful tool for a specific set
of purposes. It is not, on its own, "privacy," and treating it as a
complete answer to the questions in this course would be a mistake this
lesson is specifically trying to prevent.

## Locking Your Device

This is a habit that still matters as much as it ever did, for a
straightforward reason: a phone, tablet, or laptop that's unlocked is, for
as long as it stays that way, open to whoever is holding it — messages,
photos, email, banking apps, saved passwords, all at once. A locked device
with a passcode, pattern, fingerprint, or face unlock closes that off in
the time it takes to set your device down, and most devices will lock
automatically after a short period of inactivity if you enable that
setting.

The habit that matters here is less about the strength of the passcode
than about consistency — a lock screen that's always on, rather than one
that's been turned off for convenience and never turned back on.

---

## Not Lending an Unlocked Device

A closely related habit: handing someone your phone to make a call or look
something up, already unlocked, gives them the same open access described
above for as long as they hold it — not because most people you'd hand a
phone to are going to misuse that access, but because there's no way to
grant "just the one thing I meant to share" once the device is unlocked in
someone else's hands. Where it's practical, opening the specific app or
screen you want to share before handing the device over accomplishes the
same goal without the same exposure.

---

## Built-In Protections Are a Reasonable Baseline

Most current operating systems include meaningful built-in protection
against common threats by default — this is true across the major
platforms, not a feature of one over another, and none of them is immune;
that gap has been closing over time rather than disappearing. For most
people, the behaviors that affect your actual risk more than which device
or operating system you use are the ones covered across this module and the
next: a unique password for each important account, two-factor
authentication turned on where it's offered, keeping software updated, and
not running a file or following an instruction just because a stranger — or
an unexpected message from someone you know — told you to.

---

## In Short

1. Most web traffic today is encrypted, so the traditional public Wi-Fi warning is smaller than it used to be.
2. The sharper public-Wi-Fi risk is a fake network with a convincing name, not passive eavesdropping.
3. A VPN hides your traffic and location from your local network and provider, who are replaced, not removed, by the VPN provider; it does nothing about cookies, logins, or fingerprinting.
4. Locking your device is still a simple, high-value habit — the value is in keeping it consistently on.
5. Don't hand over an unlocked device for a narrow task if opening the specific screen first is practical.
