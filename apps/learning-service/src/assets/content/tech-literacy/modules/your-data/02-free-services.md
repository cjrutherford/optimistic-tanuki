# What "Free" Costs

A service that charges no money still has costs on its end: people to employ,
computers to run, network connections to pay for. When none of that cost is
passed to you as a price, it is being paid some other way. This lesson is
about identifying that other way, concretely, for the service in front of
you.

There are two common answers: your attention, and your data. Many services
run on both at once. Neither is a secret arrangement — it is a business
model, and it is usually identifiable if you look at how the company reports
making money.

---

## Attention as the Product

Some services are funded by advertising. The company's revenue comes from
other businesses paying to place messages in front of the service's users.
In that arrangement, the thing being sold is not the service you are using —
it is a slice of your attention, sold to an advertiser.

This changes what the service is optimized for. A service funded this way
generally benefits from you spending more time using it, since more time
using it means more opportunities to show you paid messages. That is not a
claim about any individual company's intent; it is a description of what the
revenue mechanism rewards. Features that increase how long or how often you
use something — notifications, autoplay, infinite feeds, recommendations
tuned to what keeps you engaged — are consistent with this incentive, whether
or not the effect was the original goal.

You can usually confirm this by checking how a company describes its own
revenue, in places like an investor report or a press release about earnings.
If the great majority of revenue is listed as "advertising," attention is the
product being sold, and the service is a way of assembling an audience for
that sale.

---

## Data as the Product

A second, related model sells information about users rather than access to
their attention directly. This can take the form of data shared with
advertising partners to target messages more precisely — knowing more about a
user makes the attention sold to advertisers more valuable, since a
specifically targeted audience is worth more than an undifferentiated one. It
can also take the form of data sold or licensed more directly, to data
brokers or analytics firms, for purposes unrelated to advertising at all.

The two models — attention and data — commonly combine: the data makes the
attention worth more, and the attention is what is actually invoiced to an
advertiser. Distinguishing them matters because they suggest different
questions. An attention-funded service raises the question "what is this
designed to make me keep doing." A data-funded service raises the question
"what is being recorded about me, and who else receives it."

---

## Neither Model Is Hidden, but Neither Is Obvious

Companies generally disclose what they do with data, in a privacy policy or
terms of service, because doing so is legally required in many jurisdictions.
"Hidden" is the wrong word for something written down and published. "Not
read" is more accurate — the documents are long, written in general legal
language, and rarely read by the people they describe. The next lesson in
this module covers how to read a specific privacy setting rather than an
entire policy document, which is a more practical unit to work with.

The absence of a price tag does not mean the absence of a transaction. It
means the transaction is denominated in something other than currency, and
the terms are usually written down somewhere, even if not read.

---

## Paid Alternatives Are Not Automatically Different

Paying for a service removes the need to fund it through advertising, but it
does not automatically remove data collection — a paid service can still
collect and use data, and its policy is the only reliable source for whether
it does. The presence of a price is evidence about how a service is funded,
not proof of what it does with information about its users. The same
question — what is collected, and what is it used for — applies to a paid
service as much as a free one; the answer is simply not implied by the price
the same way the free case implies advertising or data as the likely funding
source.

---

## A Practical Way to Think About a New Service

When you start using something new, it is reasonable to ask, in order: does
this cost money; if not, does the company report advertising revenue; and
does its privacy policy describe sharing data with third parties, and for
what stated purpose. None of these questions requires technical expertise —
they require reading a page most people skip, or checking a company's own
public financial disclosures if it is large enough to have any.

This is not a verdict on whether to use the service. A service funded by
advertising can still be worth using; the point is deciding with the funding
model in view rather than assuming "free" means "no cost to me at all."

---

## A Third Model: Subsidized by a Larger Business

A less discussed arrangement is a service offered free because it supports a
company's other, paid business. A free note-taking tool offered by a company
that primarily sells business software, for instance, may exist to build
familiarity with that company's products, or to gather usage patterns that
inform the paid product's development, rather than to generate advertising
revenue directly from the free tool itself. This is worth naming as a
distinct case, because it does not fit neatly into either "sells attention"
or "sells data" — the free service can be funded by the company's overall
revenue, with no separate transaction attached to the free tool at all,
though the company's privacy policy remains the only reliable source for
whether data from the free tool feeds into the paid business in some way.

Recognizing which of these three general patterns — advertising, data, or
subsidy from a paid product — applies to a given free service does not
require insider knowledge. It usually requires reading the same handful of
public documents mentioned above: how the company describes its revenue, and
what its privacy policy says about data sharing.

1. A service with no price still has costs, paid some way other than currency.
2. Advertising-funded services are generally paid for by selling your attention to advertisers.
3. Data-funded arrangements sell or use information about you, often to make that attention more valuable.
4. Companies typically disclose this in policies and financial reports; the information exists, it is just rarely read.
5. A price tag changes the funding question but does not by itself answer what data is collected.
